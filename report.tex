\begin{abstract}
We study three progressively stronger agent-construction strategies for the
SoccerTwos 2-vs-2 environment: direct PPO from scratch, behavioral cloning (BC)
from a provided baseline agent, and BC-initialized PPO fine-tuning with reward
shaping against that baseline.
Our first logged PPO baseline, trained with
\texttt{train\_ray\_team\_vs\_random.py} against a random opponent in
\texttt{team\_vs\_policy} mode, reaches an episode reward mean of 1.72 after
3.6M timesteps.
Our strongest logged fine-tuning run, produced by
\texttt{train\_ray\_bc\_finetune\_vs\_baseline\_shaped.py}, reaches a peak
episode reward mean of 1.28 at 5.04M timesteps and finishes at 0.91 after 7.2M
timesteps while training against the significantly stronger
\texttt{ceia\_baseline\_agent}.
The behavioral cloning stage provides the initialization that makes this harder
fine-tuning setting practical, while shaped rewards improve learning signal over
the sparse goal-only objective.
In a 200-episode head-to-head evaluation against
\texttt{baseline\_teacher\_agent}, Agent~1 wins 31 games (15.5\%) while
Agent~3 wins 136 games (68.0\%).
Because the repository does not contain preserved BC-only training logs or
standardized evaluation records for Agent~2, we leave explicit placeholders for
that stage rather than inserting unsupported numbers.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning, Imitation Learning, Reward Shaping,
Proximal Policy Optimization}

\section{Overview}

SoccerTwos is a physics-based 2-vs-2 soccer environment in which each player
receives a 336-dimensional observation and outputs a branched discrete action.
The default task reward is sparse: scoring yields $+1$ and conceding yields
$-1$.
This makes exploration difficult, especially when policies start from random
initialization.
To study practical ways around this problem, we organized our experiments into
three agents.
Agent~1 uses PPO trained directly in a relatively easy setting against a random
team.
Agent~2 uses behavioral cloning to imitate the provided
\texttt{ceia\_baseline\_agent}.
Agent~3 starts from BC-derived weights and then fine-tunes with PPO against the
baseline while adding dense reward shaping terms.
The first and third agents have preserved Ray training logs in the repository,
which makes them the focus of our quantitative analysis.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Agent 1 -- PPO Against a Random Opponent}

Our first reported baseline is trained by
\texttt{train\_ray\_team\_vs\_random.py} using PPO~\citep{schulman2017proximal}
in Ray RLlib~\citep{liang2018rllib}.
The environment is configured as \texttt{team\_vs\_policy} with
\texttt{multiagent=False}, so one policy jointly controls both teammates.
No explicit opponent policy is passed; under the project wrapper in
\texttt{utils.py}, this means the environment uses its default random opponent.

The PPO objective is the standard clipped surrogate:
\begin{equation}
  L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) =
    \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[
      \min\!\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\;
      \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t
      \right)
    \right],
\end{equation}
where $r_t(\theta) =
\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)$.
In our logged run, the policy uses two fully connected hidden layers of size
512 and is trained on CPU with 8 workers and 3 vectorized environments per
worker.

\subsection{Agent 2 -- Behavioral Cloning from the Baseline Agent}

The BC agent is produced by the repository's observation-normalized distillation
pipeline in \texttt{distill\_bc\_obs.py}, and packaged in
\texttt{baseline\_bc\_agent/}.
This stage is supervised imitation learning rather than reinforcement learning.
The script collects 300 episodes of expert data from the provided
\texttt{ceia\_baseline\_agent}, computes per-dimension observation mean and
standard deviation, normalizes each observation with
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{s}_d = \frac{s_d - \mu_d}{\sigma_d + 10^{-8}},
\end{equation}
and trains a two-layer MLP with three action heads using cross-entropy loss.

This observation normalization is a meaningful design choice because the raw
336-dimensional observation mixes features on different scales, such as ray-cast
fractions and physical velocities.
The trained BC checkpoint is saved to \texttt{bc\_obs\_0/checkpoint.pth} and can
be loaded directly by the packaged inference agent.

\subsection{Agent 3 -- BC-Initialized PPO with Reward Shaping}

Our final reported fine-tuning experiment corresponds to the logged run
\texttt{PPO\_bc\_finetune\_vs\_baseline\_shaped\_lr2e5\_clip01\_3p6M}, which is
produced by
\texttt{train\_ray\_bc\_finetune\_vs\_baseline\_shaped.py}.
This is important: although the repository also contains a simpler exploratory
\texttt{train\_reward\_shaping.py}, the preserved result analyzed in this report
comes from the baseline-vs-shaped fine-tuning script, not that earlier
exploration.

In this setup, the learning policy controls the blue team in a
\texttt{multiagent\_player} environment, while the opponent is the fixed
\texttt{ceia\_baseline\_agent}.
The PPO model is initialized from a BC checkpoint
(\texttt{bc\_agent/checkpoint.pth}) by copying the shared hidden layers and
concatenating the three BC action heads into the single RLlib logits layer.
This gives PPO a non-random starting policy before any policy-gradient update.

\paragraph{Reward shaping.}
The shaped environment adds dense terms to the sparse per-goal reward.
The implementation in
\texttt{train\_ray\_bc\_finetune\_vs\_baseline\_shaped.py} includes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ball-progress potential.}
    The script predicts the future ball position using the current velocity and
    rewards the increase in normalized forward ball position:
    \[
      r^{\text{prog}} = 0.02\left(\Phi_t - \Phi_{t-1}\right), \quad
      \Phi_t = \hat{x}_t / 14.
    \]
    This is the component most closely aligned with potential-based shaping
    theory~\citep{ng1999policy}.
  \item \textbf{Ball-contest reward.}
    The player nearest to the predicted ball position receives a small positive
    bonus based on an exponential distance decay.
  \item \textbf{Defensive coverage.}
    When the ball is in the team's own half, the team receives a bonus if at
    least one defender is positioned between the ball and the home goal along the
    ball lane, and a small penalty otherwise.
  \item \textbf{Support positioning.}
    The non-nearest teammate is rewarded for maintaining width and depth behind
    the ball in attack, or for covering the goal lane in defense.
  \item \textbf{Anti-crowding and lateral-separation terms.}
    A penalty discourages both teammates from collapsing to the same point, while
    a small bonus rewards useful lateral spacing.
\end{enumerate}

Unlike a later experimental branch in the repository, this specific logged run
does \emph{not} randomize team side each episode and does \emph{not} apply a
global shaping scale factor such as $\alpha=0.15$.
Those details therefore should not be attributed to the result analyzed here.

\section{Analysis and Discussion}

\paragraph{Agent 1 learns a usable PPO baseline.}
Our earlier sparse-reward experiments against weaker or more static opponents were
useful for debugging, but the clean logged baseline used in this report is the
\texttt{PPO\_team\_vs\_random} run.
In that setting, PPO from random initialization is able to learn meaningful team
behavior.
The final reward mean reaches 1.72 at 3.6M timesteps, and the mean episode length
drops sharply from 398 at the beginning of training to about 103 by the final
iteration.
This suggests the policy learns to end episodes more decisively, likely by
scoring earlier and avoiding the long random drift seen at initialization.
However, when evaluated against the much stronger
\texttt{baseline\_teacher\_agent}, this policy wins only 31 out of 200 games,
showing that competence against a random opponent does not automatically transfer
to a stronger adversary.

\paragraph{Agent 3 solves a harder problem than Agent 1.}
Although Agent~3's final reward mean (0.91) is numerically lower than Agent~1's,
this does not imply that Agent~1 is the stronger agent overall.
Agent~1 is trained against a default random opponent in a simpler
\texttt{team\_vs\_policy} setting, while Agent~3 is trained against the fixed
pre-trained baseline in a custom multi-agent wrapper.
The more meaningful reading of Agent~3's curve is that BC initialization plus
reward shaping makes it possible to learn nontrivial behavior in a much more
difficult training regime.

\paragraph{Behavioral cloning is the bridge between the two stages.}
The repository's BC pipeline in \texttt{distill\_bc\_obs.py} is the mechanism that
makes Agent~3 plausible.
Instead of starting PPO from random weights against the baseline, the final agent
begins from a policy already aligned with expert baseline actions.
Even though we do not have the original BC training logs in the repository, the
checkpoint structure and loader code show that the BC stage preserves both network
weights and observation-normalization statistics for downstream reuse.
This is a practical example of imitation learning reducing the exploration burden
before policy-gradient fine-tuning.

\paragraph{Reward shaping helps, but the final checkpoint is not always the best checkpoint.}
The shaped fine-tuning run improves from an initial reward mean of 0.24 to a peak
of 1.28 around 5.04M timesteps, then declines to 0.91 by the final 7.2M-timestep
checkpoint.
This matters for packaging and evaluation: the best policy for submission may be a
mid-training checkpoint such as \texttt{checkpoint-630}, not the final saved
model.
This is exactly the kind of behavior for which checkpointed Ray training is
useful, because it lets us select by measured validation signal rather than by
training duration alone.
Even with the currently packaged checkpoint, the shaped fine-tuned agent wins 136
out of 200 games against \texttt{baseline\_teacher\_agent}, which is a clear
improvement over Agent~1 in this direct comparison.

\paragraph{Observation normalization is well motivated even when logs are missing.}
The BC distillation script normalizes all 336 observation dimensions before
supervised training.
Given that the observation mixes values on very different scales, this is a
reasonable engineering choice and consistent with broader deep RL practice
~\citep{andrychowicz2021matters}.
Because the normalization statistics are saved with the BC checkpoint and applied
again at inference time, the modification is not just theoretical; it is an
implemented part of the training-to-deployment pipeline.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our main limitation is artifact completeness rather than lack of ideas.
The repository preserves two full Ray training runs and several packaged agents,
but not a consistent evaluation suite or the BC-only learning curves.
As a result, we can make strong claims about how the logged PPO runs behaved
during training, but only weaker claims about final head-to-head playing strength.
Future iterations should standardize evaluation runs for every major checkpoint
and save those summaries alongside the training curves.

\section{Conclusion}

This project produced a clear progression of agent-building strategies for
SoccerTwos.
Direct PPO against a random opponent yields a solid baseline and demonstrates that
the repository's CPU-only RLlib setup can learn coordinated team behavior.
Behavioral cloning then provides a practical way to transfer the provided baseline
agent's behavior into a student policy.
Finally, BC-initialized PPO with reward shaping enables training against a much
stronger opponent and achieves its best performance at an intermediate checkpoint,
highlighting the importance of both initialization and checkpoint selection.
The strongest empirical claims in this report come from the preserved Ray logs,
and those logs support the overall conclusion that imitation learning and reward
shaping are useful tools for tackling sparse-reward multi-agent soccer.
